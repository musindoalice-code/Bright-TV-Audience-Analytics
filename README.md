## 📺 30-Second Read

> **1,523 hours watched. 10,000 sessions. 4,386 subscribers. Two of nine provinces drive 56% of it.**
> Sport — not general entertainment — is the single biggest lever in the schedule. And two "obvious" patterns in the data (a 90.7% male audience, "weekday-heavy" viewing) turn out to need a second look before anyone acts on them.

I took Bright TV's subscriber demographics and Q1 2016 viewing logs through Databricks SQL, then delivered the same analysis across five BI platforms — Lovable, Power BI, Google Data Studio, Excel, and Databricks — so it reaches whichever stakeholder is reviewing it.

## 💡 Key Insights

1. **Gauteng dominates reach.** It alone drives 36.3% of total watch hours — nearly double the runner-up. Paired with Western Cape, the two most urbanised provinces account for 56.0% of all viewing; Northern Cape sits at just 1.8%, the lightest reach in the country.
2. **Sport is the core draw, not a category among many.** The ICC Cricket World Cup and Live Events together capture 48.0% of all viewing hours — sport clearly outperforms general entertainment as a driver of watch time.
3. **The audience skews young, and sessions are short.** Youth and Adult segments make up 89.3% of viewing hours, and average session length is just 9.1 minutes — a signature of channel-surfing and sampling behaviour rather than long, committed viewing.
4. **Afternoons and mornings own the schedule.** These two slots account for 68.1% of all viewing hours combined, while Midnight remains largely dormant at 7.0% — a clear signal for where to place flagship content versus reruns.
5. **A long tail of underused channels exists.** 10 of the 17 channels aired combined contribute just 11.3% of total viewing hours — a strong candidate list for schedule refresh or rationalisation.
6. **Two "obvious" patterns don't hold up to scrutiny — and that's the point.** The data shows 90.7% of recorded viewing hours are male, but that's either a genuine skew or a data-capture issue upstream, and it needs validating before it drives ad targeting. Similarly, the "weekday-heavy" viewing split (69.5%) is close to what you'd expect by chance alone (~71.4% of days in the period are weekdays) — so on its own, it isn't actually a meaningful finding. Catching this before it reached a stakeholder deck is the difference between reporting a number and interpreting it.

## 🚀 Strategic Recommendations

| # | Recommendation | Why (evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Invest behind sport and live events** — prioritise broadcast rights, promotion, and scheduling around these formats | Cricket and live-event coverage drive nearly half of all viewing hours |
| 2 | **Prioritise Gauteng and Western Cape** in regional marketing and ad sales, while testing growth plays in under-indexed provinces | These two provinces generate 56% of total watch time |
| 3 | **Rebalance the channel line-up** — a strong candidate list for schedule refresh or channel rationalisation | 10 of 17 channels combined contribute barely a tenth of total hours |
| 4 | **Investigate white space in underrepresented audiences** (women, older and younger viewers) *after* validating the gender data | Only ~11% of recorded hours come from Kids/Teens/Elder/Pensioner combined, and gender skew needs a data-quality check first |
| 5 | **Schedule flagship content for midday** — anchor premium/premiere content in Afternoon and Morning slots, use Midnight for reruns or lower-cost programming | Afternoon and Morning draw 68% of hours combined |

**Bottom line:** the audience, the content, and the time slots that matter are all already visible in the data — the highest-value next step isn't more analysis, it's acting on what's concentrated, while fixing the data-quality gaps (gender flag, the weekday framing) before they're trusted for targeting decisions.

## 🎯 Business Problem

Bright TV faces challenges around subscriber churn and low engagement during certain broadcasting periods. Management needs a clearer view of how different subscriber groups interact with television content so that they can make better decisions around programming, advertising, and customer engagement.

The challenge was to turn the available data into useful business intelligence that could answer questions such as:

- Who are Bright TV's main viewers?
- Which television channels attract the largest audiences?
- When are viewers most likely to watch television?
- Which provinces show the highest levels of engagement?
- Which customer segments could benefit from targeted marketing campaigns?
- How can Bright TV improve customer retention and viewer engagement?

This project uses SQL and Business Intelligence techniques to explore these questions and turn the findings into practical recommendations.

---

## 🎯 Project Objectives

The main objectives of this project were to:

- Identify key factors influencing subscriber viewership and drop-offs.
- Segment audience demographics to support targeted advertising campaigns.
- Carry out a comprehensive data quality assessment.
- Identify and resolve missing or inconsistent values.
- Prepare clean datasets for analysis.
- Create new business-focused features.
- Analyse subscriber demographics and television viewing behaviour.
- Calculate executive-level KPIs.
- Prepare interactive, dashboard-ready datasets across multiple BI tools.
- Generate business recommendations supported by the data.

## 🗂️ Data Model

The analysis connects subscriber demographic information to viewing behaviour using the subscriber ID.

```text
┌──────────────────────────────┐
│   subscriber_demographics     │
├──────────────────────────────┤
│ UserID (Primary Key)          │
│ Region                        │
│ age / age_groups              │
│ Race                          │
│ Gender                        │
│ email_flag / sm_flag          │
└──────────────┬────────────────┘
               │
               │ UserID = sub_id
               ▼
┌──────────────────────────────┐
│      viewing_behaviour        │
├──────────────────────────────┤
│ sub_id (Foreign Key)          │
│ watch_date, month_id          │
│ Tv_channel                    │
│ time_of_day / hour_of_day     │
│ duration                      │
│ day_classification            │
└──────────────────────────────┘
```
